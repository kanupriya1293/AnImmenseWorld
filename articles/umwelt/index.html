Vision has a speed.
And we are not the fastest.

Light travels almost instantaneously. But seeing is not the same as light arriving — and different animals process what they see at very different speeds. The same room, the same lamp, experienced in entirely different ways.

14 April 2026

We take it for granted that we see the world as it is. The lamp on your desk glows steadily. The fly on your wall sits perfectly still until, somehow, it isn't. But what if the reality you experience is not the world itself — just your brain's best reconstruction of it, built at the speed your visual system allows?

When light hits your retina, a neuron fires. That signal travels along the optic nerve to your brain. Your brain then assembles thousands of such signals into a coherent scene — and only then do you see something. That entire chain takes time. The rate at which your brain can process successive images — the point at which flickering stops feeling like flickering and becomes smooth, continuous motion — is called your Critical Flicker Fusion frequency, or CFF. For humans, it is around 60 Hz.

Different animals have very different CFFs. And this single fact has some remarkable consequences for how different creatures experience the exact same world.

What you are seeing is not the bulb changing — it is physically identical throughout. What changes is the observer. The same light, experienced through different eyes, at different visual speeds.

Notice the direction: as you move down toward faster-vision animals, the bulb flickers more slowly. That is not a mistake. A housefly's visual system processes time so finely that the 100 Hz bulb — fast enough to appear continuous to us — looks obviously, slowly blinking to them. We will come back to why that matters.

Why does the bulb flicker at all?

A standard light bulb running on AC electricity does not produce continuous light. The current alternates direction 50 times per second in most countries (60 in the US), and the bulb completes a full bright–dark–bright cycle twice per AC cycle — which means it actually flickers at 100 Hz (or 120 Hz in the US).

For humans, 100 Hz is above our CFF of ~60 Hz. Our visual system cannot resolve individual flashes at that rate and averages them into what feels like constant light. The bulb appears steady. It is not.

For a housefly, whose CFF is around 250 Hz, 100 Hz falls well below their threshold. They see each flash individually — the way you would see a light blinking a few times a second. Every room lit by artificial light is, to a fly, permanently and visibly flickering.

This difference in visual speed is what the German biologist Jakob von Uexküll called the Umwelt — the idea that every organism inhabits its own perceptual world, constructed from what its particular sensory system can detect. A fly is not experiencing a degraded version of our reality. It is experiencing its own, shaped by 300 million years of insect evolution. The bulb is the same. The Umwelt is not.

Three things this changes how I think about

The bulb is a neat demonstration. But the implications of visual speed differences go further than a flickering lamp.

01

Your pet bird is living under a constant strobe

Parrots, canaries, and most pet birds have a CFF significantly higher than ours — typically in the 100–150 Hz range. A standard fluorescent or LED light that appears perfectly steady to you is visibly flickering to them. Research suggests this is a genuine stressor. Captive birds exposed to flickering artificial lighting show measurably higher stress behaviours — feather-plucking, agitation, repetitive movements — compared to birds kept under full-spectrum or natural light. Many owners attribute these behaviours to boredom or personality. The more likely explanation is that their living room is, from the bird's perspective, flickering incessantly.

02

When you try to swat a fly, it watches your hand arrive in slow motion

A housefly's CFF of ~250 Hz means it processes the world at roughly four times our temporal resolution. Motion that looks fast to us — a swinging hand, a rolled newspaper — unfolds across many more perceptual frames in the fly's visual system. It is not physically faster than us in any mechanical sense. But its perception of time is finer-grained, giving it significantly more visual information per second to detect and react to incoming threats. This is a large part of why flies are so difficult to catch. They are not clairvoyant. They simply have more time — perceptual time — to see what is coming.

03

Visual speed and movement speed are not a coincidence

Look again at the organism list: snail and sloth at the slow end, dragonfly and housefly at the fast end. This maps almost perfectly onto how fast each animal moves and needs to react in the world. A dragonfly intercepting another insect mid-flight needs to process motion at high speed or it misses. A snail, moving slowly across a leaf, has no such requirement — and paying the ongoing energetic cost of a faster visual system would bring no return. Evolution is ruthlessly efficient: no animal develops faster vision than its body and lifestyle actually need. Vision speed, it turns out, is not a measure of sophistication. It is a measure of how fast your world moves.

How the visualisation works

The flickering bulb is a simulation, not a literal replication of animal vision. To make another species' experience visible to human eyes, we use the following approach: if a bulb flickers at a certain fraction of an animal's CFF, we show it to you at that same fraction of your CFF. The relative perceptual position is preserved — scaled through your own visual system.

Simulated Hz = (Bulb Hz ÷ Observer CFF) × Human CFF (60)

So for a housefly (CFF 250 Hz) looking at a 100 Hz bulb: (100 ÷ 250) × 60 = 24 Hz. A 24 Hz flicker is slow and obvious to any human — which is precisely the point.

Because a 60 Hz screen cannot faithfully display frequencies above 30 Hz (its Nyquist limit), the animation is proportionally scaled down to keep all flickering organisms renderable and in the correct order. The formula values shown in the widget are the scientifically accurate ones.

This is an approximation. CFF varies between individuals, changes with light levels and fatigue, and the full experience of animal vision involves far more than flicker rate — eye structure, spectral sensitivity, field of view, and neural processing all differ dramatically across species. What the simulation captures is one dimension: temporal resolution. That one dimension turns out to be enough to make the point.
