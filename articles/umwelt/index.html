Umwelt & Critical Flicker Fusion

Not every eye inhabits the same moment.

The Umwelt

In 1909, the Baltic German biologist Jakob von Uexküll proposed that each animal lives inside its own private sensory world — its Umwelt, from the German for surrounding world. His claim was radical: there is no single, shared, objective reality that all creatures inhabit. Each animal constructs its own version of the world from the signals its senses can detect, filtered through the nervous system evolution gave it.

A tick, nearly blind and deaf, navigates existence via three signals alone: the smell of butyric acid from mammalian skin, warmth, and the texture of hair. It may wait years — even decades — for these signals to arrive. When they do, its Umwelt is complete. Everything else that happens around it simply does not exist within its experience. The tick does not perceive an impoverished world. It perceives its world, perfectly and wholly.

Ed Yong, in An Immense World (2022), surveys the astonishing range of animal sensory experience — from the electric fields detected by sharks, to the polarised light read by mantis shrimp, to the magnetic maps used by sea turtles. What emerges is a kind of humility: the world as we experience it is not the world as it is. It is a model, tuned by millions of years of evolution, for what mattered to our lineage.

The Speed of Sight

One axis along which Umwelts diverge sharply is time. Vision is not instantaneous — the eye integrates light over a brief interval, and a sufficiently rapid flicker is averaged away into the perception of steady illumination. The frequency at which this happens is the Critical Flicker Fusion frequency, or CFF.

A standard incandescent lamp driven by a 50 Hz alternating current flickers at 100 Hz — the element brightens twice per AC cycle, once in each direction of current flow. Humans, with a CFF of approximately 60 Hz, cannot resolve this; the light appears perfectly continuous. We are, in the temporal sense, too slow to notice.

A housefly (Musca domestica), however, has a CFF of around 250 Hz. For the fly, that same domestic bulb pulses like a lazy, obvious strobe. This is not a defect in the fly's vision — it is a profound advantage. Tracking the rapid wing-beat of a rival, or reacting to the loom of a threat, demands temporal resolution we can barely imagine. A pigeon at 145 Hz, a dragonfly at 200 Hz: each perceives a different version of the same flickering world.

To translate one species' experience into human-visible terms, we can apply a simple proportional formula. If the bulb's flicker represents a particular fraction of the animal's CFF, we scale that same fraction to human CFF:

Simulated Hz = (Bulb Hz ÷ Observer CFF) × Human CFF

e.g. Housefly: (100 ÷ 250) × 60 = 24 Hz — a slow, clear strobe
e.g. Ground squirrel: (100 ÷ 120) × 60 = 50 Hz — a noticeable flicker

When the derived frequency reaches or exceeds 60 Hz, the simulation would be imperceptible to humans anyway — so we show a steady bulb. This applies to species whose CFF falls at or below the bulb's own frequency of 100 Hz.

    Species whose CFF falls below the bulb's 100 Hz — snails, sloths, tortoises, humans, dogs, cats — all perceive the same steady light. Their temporal resolution is simply not fine enough to detect the flicker. For them, the lamp glows continuously, as though connected to direct current.

    Species with a CFF above 100 Hz experience something entirely different. The ground squirrel at 120 Hz, the pigeon at 145 Hz, the dragonfly at 200 Hz, the housefly at 250 Hz — each sees a bulb that blinks. The higher the CFF, the more obvious the strobe, and the lower the simulated frequency falls when mapped onto human perception.

    The dragonfly is worth a moment's pause. With a CFF near 200 Hz and compound eyes covering almost the entire sphere of space around its head, it tracks and intercepts prey mid-flight with success rates above 95%. Its temporal world is sliced into finer intervals than ours. What appears to us as a continuous blur of wing-beats is, to the dragonfly, a sequence of distinct, readable frames.

    Jakob von Uexküll described each Umwelt as a soap bubble — complete, sealed, enclosing its own private reality. The bulb above flickers or glows steady not according to the laws of physics, but according to biology. Same photons. Different realities.
