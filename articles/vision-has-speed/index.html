Umwelt

Vision has a speed.
And we are not the fastest.

14 April 2026

Light travels almost instantaneously. But seeing takes time. Between light entering your eye and the world appearing in your mind, your visual system has to detect, transmit, and assemble information into a coherent scene. That takes time.

We rarely notice this, because our version of reality feels seamless. But what you experience is not the world as it is. It is your brain's best reconstruction of it, built at the speed your visual system allows. And different organisms run that process at very different speeds.

One way to measure visual speed is through something called Critical Flicker Fusion frequency, or CFF. Humans are not especially fast. Many other organisms process visual change much more quickly. Consider the lamp on your desk.

As you move toward faster-vision organisms, the bulb appears to flicker more slowly. That is not a mistake. A housefly processes time so finely that a 100 Hz bulb, fast enough to look steady to us, looks obviously and continuously blinking to it. We will come back to why that matters.

What is actually happening here?

A standard AC bulb does not produce continuous light. It flickers at 100 Hz in most countries, and 120 Hz in the US. Whether that flicker is visible or not depends on an animal's CFF: the point above which flickering light stops looking like flicker and starts looking continuous. For humans, that threshold is around 60 Hz, so the 100 Hz flashes of a light bulb fuse into smooth, continuous light.

For a housefly, whose CFF is around 250 Hz, that same bulb falls well below its threshold. The fly can resolve each flash individually, the way you would notice a light blinking a few times a second. Every artificially lit room is, to a fly, visibly flickering.

That does not mean the fly is experiencing a degraded version of our reality. It is experiencing its own, shaped by millions of years of evolution. The bulb is the same. The perceptual world around it is not.

In the early 1900s, the German biologist Jakob von Uexküll proposed something quietly radical: every organism inhabits its own perceptual world, or 'umwelt', built from what its senses can detect. Our senses give us one version of reality. Another organism's senses give it another. What we experience is not the world itself, but the world as we are equipped to perceive it. Every creature mistakes its own Umwelt for the truth.

What the Lamp Reveals About Other Worlds

A flickering bulb feels like a neat trick. But it points to something much stranger: animals are not all moving through the same visible world at the same pace. For some, your home may pulse with light. For others, your fastest movement may unfold with more warnings than you realize. What looks to us like one shared reality is actually split into different perceptual speeds.

Once you see that, ordinary things stop being ordinary. The agitated pet bird, the fly that always escapes, a sloth's slowness — none of these are random quirks. They are consequences of living in different perceptual worlds.

01

Your bird's personality may be your lighting

Parrots, canaries, and many other pet birds process visual information faster than we do, which means that light that feels steady to us is likely flickering to them all the time. Research suggests that artificial lighting can be a genuine source of stress in captive birds, linked to agitation, feather-plucking, and repetitive behaviour. What owners dismiss as temperament or mood is likely because the room is pulsating incessantly for the bird.

02

The fly saw it coming long before you think

A housefly processes the world at roughly four times our temporal resolution. A swinging hand or rolled newspaper does not arrive as one sudden blur for houseflies. It unfolds across many more perceptual frames, giving the fly more time to detect the threat and react. That is part of why flies are so difficult to catch. They are not performing a last-second miracle. They simply see your move in slow motion.

03

A sloth is not slow because it is lazy

If a sloth tried to move faster, the world would blur past faster than it could resolve, and it would crash. Since a sloth lives on leaves, it does not need speed to chase prey or make split-second escapes, so evolution slowed its muscles and visual system together. Over time, it lost the ability to move any faster because it did not need to. A dragonfly, on the other hand, needs rapid visual updates to intercept prey mid-flight or it misses. Fast vision is metabolically expensive, and evolution is very efficient: it does not pay for abilities an organism has no use for. Vision speed is thus not a measure of intelligence or sophistication. It is tuned, quite precisely, to the pace of life an organism actually lives.

04

Slow and fast belong to our world, not theirs

What we call slow and fast are not universal truths. They are judgments made from within our own Umwelt. In another animal's Umwelt, we may be a flickering blur, much as a fly is in ours.

How this was simulated

The flickering bulb is a simulation, not a literal recreation of animal vision. To make another species' experience visible to human eyes, the visualisation preserves relative perceptual position: if a bulb flickers at a certain fraction of an animal's CFF, it is shown to you at that same fraction of your own CFF.

Simulated Hz = (Bulb Hz ÷ Observer CFF) × Human CFF (60)

So for a housefly, with a CFF of 250 Hz, looking at a 100 Hz bulb:

(100 ÷ 250) × 60 = 24 Hz

A 24 Hz flicker looks slow and obvious to us. That is the point: not to reproduce fly vision literally, but to preserve where the bulb sits relative to the fly's visual speed. The values shown in the widget, however, are the organism's actual CFFs.

Because a 60 Hz screen cannot faithfully display flicker above 30 Hz, the animation is scaled down proportionally so that it remains viewable on a standard digital screen.

This is still only an approximation. CFF varies across individuals and conditions, and vision involves far more than flicker rate: eye structure, spectral sensitivity, field of view, and neural processing all differ across species. The simulation captures just one dimension, temporal resolution, but even that one dimension is enough to make the point.

The visualisation was built with AI, as part of a broader interest in using AI not just to retrieve information, but to turn it into something visible and interactive.
